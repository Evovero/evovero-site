<p>Negative keywords stop your Google Ads from showing for searches that will never turn into a job. For a local service business, the fastest way to use them is to open the search terms report every week, add the irrelevant searches as negatives, and keep a shared list of the terms that never convert, like job seekers and do-it-yourself searches.</p>

<p>I look at service-business accounts regularly, and the same thing shows up again and again: money going to searches from people who were never going to hire anyone. This post walks through how I think about negatives, how the match types actually behave, and a simple weekly routine you can run in about fifteen minutes.</p>

<h2>What a Negative Keyword Actually Does</h2>
<p>A negative keyword tells Google not to show your ad when a search includes that term. Google's own example is an optometrist who sells eyeglasses and excludes "wine glasses." You are not choosing who sees your ad so much as removing the searches you know are a bad fit. Google describes this in its <a href="https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/2453972" target="_blank" rel="noopener">negative keywords help page</a>.</p>
<p>For a local trade, the bad-fit searches are predictable. A tree service gets people searching for tree jobs. A concrete contractor gets people searching for how to mix concrete themselves. A towing company gets people searching for tow truck driver training. None of those searchers is a customer, and each click costs you something.</p>

<h2>Start With the Search Terms Report</h2>
<p>Your keyword list is what you asked for. The search terms report is what people actually typed. Google's <a href="https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/2472708" target="_blank" rel="noopener">search terms report page</a> explains that it shows the actual searches that triggered your ads and how those searches performed, and that you can add irrelevant ones as negative keywords so the ad stops showing for them.</p>
<p>One detail matters here: the report does not add negatives for you. You read it, decide, and make the change in your account. That is the whole job, and it is why a weekly habit beats an occasional cleanup.</p>

<h3>What I look for each week</h3>
<ul>
<li>Searches that show a different intent, such as jobs, training, salary, or classes.</li>
<li>Do-it-yourself searches: "how to," "diy," "tutorial," or "rent a" something.</li>
<li>Searches for a service you do not offer, which broad keywords love to attract.</li>
<li>Searches from the wrong place, such as a neighboring state or a town outside your service area.</li>
<li>Anything with clicks and no calls or form fills over several weeks.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Know the Three Match Types Before You Add Anything</h2>
<p>This is where most owners get burned. Negative keywords do not behave like regular keywords, and Google's help page spells out the difference.</p>

<h3>Broad match negatives</h3>
<p>This is the default. The ad is blocked only when the search includes all of your negative terms, in any order. If you exclude "running shoes," the search "shoes running" is blocked, but "running shoe" is not. A broad negative of two words will not block a search that has only one of them.</p>

<h3>Phrase match negatives</h3>
<p>The ad is blocked when the search contains your terms in the same order, with other words allowed around them. Excluding "running shoes" this way blocks "blue running shoes" but not "shoes running." I use phrase match for most of my service-business negatives, because the searches I want gone usually contain a phrase like "tree removal jobs."</p>

<h3>Exact match negatives</h3>
<p>The ad is blocked only when the search is exactly those terms in that order with nothing added. This is the narrowest option. It is useful when a term is fine in most contexts but bad in one specific search.</p>

<h3>Variants are on you</h3>
<p>Per the same page, negatives do not exclude close variants or other expansions. Excluding "flowers" does not block "red flower." For Search campaigns you have to add singular and plural forms and synonyms yourself. If you add "job" as a negative and forget "jobs," you will keep paying for the second one.</p>

<h2>Build a Shared List So You Do Not Start Over</h2>
<p>Every local service account ends up needing the same core negatives. Google describes account-level negative keyword lists as applying automatically to eligible Search and Shopping inventory in the relevant campaign types, which means you can build the list once and not repeat the work campaign by campaign.</p>
<p>A starting list for most trades looks like this:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Job seekers:</strong> jobs, hiring, careers, employment, salary, apply.</li>
<li><strong>Training:</strong> classes, course, school, certification, training.</li>
<li><strong>Do it yourself:</strong> diy, how to, tutorial, instructions.</li>
<li><strong>Freebies:</strong> free, sample, coupon, for free.</li>
<li><strong>Research only:</strong> definition, wikipedia, history of.</li>
</ul>
<p>Then add negatives that are specific to your trade. A masonry company might add terms for products it does not install. A towing company might exclude parts and equipment searches. Keep the trade-specific terms in the campaign they belong to, so a negative that protects one service does not accidentally block another.</p>

<h2>The Mistake That Costs You Real Jobs</h2>
<p>Negatives can hurt you if you are careless. The classic example is adding a broad-sounding word that also appears in good searches. If you excluded "cheap" because you do not want bargain hunters, you might also block someone searching for a fast, affordable fix who would have booked. Before adding a term, ask whether any real customer could plausibly type it.</p>
<p>My rule is simple: when in doubt, use phrase or exact match instead of a single broad word. A narrow negative removes the clear waste and leaves the borderline searches to be judged by their results over a few weeks.</p>

<h2>A Fifteen-Minute Weekly Routine</h2>
<ol>
<li>Open the search terms report for the last seven days.</li>
<li>Sort by clicks and read the top searches that did not lead to a call or form.</li>
<li>Pick the clearly irrelevant ones and add them as phrase match negatives, plus the plural or singular form.</li>
<li>Add any new generally useless term to your shared list so every campaign benefits.</li>
<li>Leave borderline searches alone until they have had a few weeks to prove themselves.</li>
<li>Check that your calls and form leads are still tracked, so you are judging searches by real results.</li>
</ol>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>

<h3>How often should I add negative keywords?</h3>
<p>Weekly is the right pace for a new or changing account. Read the search terms report, add the clearly irrelevant searches, and move on. Once the account settles, a quick check every couple of weeks is usually enough to catch new junk searches before they add up.</p>

<h3>Should I use broad, phrase, or exact match for negatives?</h3>
<p>I default to phrase match for most service-business negatives. A broad negative only blocks searches containing every term you listed, and an exact negative blocks only the identical search. Phrase match catches the common patterns without being as narrow as exact or as easy to miss as broad.</p>

<h3>Do negative keywords block plurals and misspellings?</h3>
<p>Google handles casing and misspellings for you, but negatives do not exclude close variants or other expansions. You need to add the singular and plural forms and any synonyms yourself. If you exclude "job" and not "jobs," searches with the second will still trigger your ad.</p>

<h3>Can negative keywords hurt my campaign?</h3>
<p>Yes, if you add a term that real customers also type. A word like "cheap" or "emergency" can show up in good searches. Use phrase or exact match for borderline terms, and check results after a few weeks before deciding a term belongs on the permanent list.</p>

<h3>Do I need a shared negative keyword list?</h3>
<p>It saves time. Google applies account-level negative lists automatically to eligible Search and Shopping inventory in relevant campaign types, so you build your job-seeker, training, and do-it-yourself terms once. Trade-specific negatives stay in the campaign they protect so they do not block other services.</p>
