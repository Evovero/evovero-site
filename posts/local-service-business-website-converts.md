<p>A local service business website converts when a visitor can tell within a few seconds what you do, where you do it, and how to reach you, and then reach you in one tap. Everything else is secondary. Most of the sites I look at fail on those three things before design, copy, or SEO ever enters the conversation.</p>

<p>I want to be specific about what that looks like in practice, because "your website should convert better" is advice nobody can act on. Here is what I actually check, in the order I check it.</p>

<h2>The Three Questions Every Visitor Arrives With</h2>

<p>Someone lands on your site because they have a problem right now. A cracked driveway, a tree on the garage, a truck that will not start. They are not browsing. They are qualifying you in about five seconds, and they are asking three things.</p>

<p>Do you do this specific thing? Not "concrete services" but the thing they typed into Google. Do you work where I live? A visitor in a suburb twenty minutes from your shop needs to see their town named, not a radius they have to guess at. How do I reach you? Phone number, form, or text, visible without scrolling.</p>

<p>If your homepage headline is your company name and a tagline, you have answered none of the three. The fix is usually one sentence and a service area line, and it moves more leads than a redesign.</p>

<h2>Speed Is a Conversion Feature, Not a Technical One</h2>

<p>Site speed gets treated as a developer concern. It is a revenue concern. A visitor on a phone with a spotty signal who waits several seconds for your hero image to render is a visitor who hit back and called the next contractor in the results.</p>

<p>Google's Core Web Vitals give you a usable target, and the thresholds have been steady. Largest Contentful Paint under 2.5 seconds is considered good, Interaction to Next Paint under 200 milliseconds, and Cumulative Layout Shift under 0.1. Those are measured against real Chrome users visiting your site, at the 75th percentile over a rolling 28-day window, which is worth understanding: it is not a lab score you can game with a one-time test. It is what your actual visitors on their actual phones experienced.</p>

<p>I have seen sites with beautiful design fail here because of one uncompressed hero photo and four tracking scripts nobody remembers installing. If you have never checked, check. It is free, and it is often the single cheapest lead increase available to a local business.</p>

<h2>Your Form Is Almost Certainly Too Long</h2>

<p>Every field you add to a contact form is a place someone quits. I see forms asking for full address, property type, budget range, preferred contact time, and how they heard about you, all before the business has even talked to the person.</p>

<p>You do not need any of that to call someone back. Name, phone, and a short description of the problem is enough to have a real conversation, and you can collect everything else during that conversation. The information you think you are gathering with a long form is information you are trading leads for.</p>

<p>Two related things matter as much as length. The form has to work cleanly on a phone, with tap targets big enough to hit and the right keyboard appearing for a phone number field. And submissions have to actually arrive somewhere you will see them, ideally routed into a system rather than an email address you check at night. Plenty of businesses have a form quietly dumping into a spam folder, and the site looks fine the whole time.</p>

<h2>The Phone Number Problem</h2>

<p>Most people calling a service business are calling from the same phone they are browsing on. If your number is set as an image, or as text they have to copy, you have added friction to the highest-intent action on your site.</p>

<p>Make it a tap-to-call link, put it in the header where it stays visible, and repeat it at the end of every service page. Then answer it. I am not being glib. <h2>Service Pages Beat a Services List</h2>

<p>A single page listing eight services with a paragraph each is a page that ranks for nothing and convinces nobody. Each real service you offer deserves its own page that goes deep: what the work involves, what the process looks like, what a customer should expect, what questions they always ask.</p>

<p>This does double duty. It gives search engines something specific to match against a specific query, and it gives a visitor enough substance to trust you before they call. That second part is what people miss. Depth reads as competence. A thin page reads as a business that might not know much more about the job than you do.</p>

<p>The same logic applies to where you work. A page for each town you serve, with real local detail rather than the same paragraph with the town name swapped, is how you show up for searches that include a place name. <h2>Proof Where the Decision Happens</h2>

<p>Reviews, photos of your own work, and any credential you actually hold belong near the point of decision, not stranded on an About page nobody visits. A row of recent reviews next to your contact form does more than a testimonials page ever will.</p>

<p>Use your own photos. Stock images of someone else's job site are recognizable, and a visitor who spots one starts wondering what else is not real. Your phone takes fine photos. Take them.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>

<h3>How do I know if my website is actually losing leads?</h3>
<p>Start with three checks. Run your site through a Core Web Vitals test on mobile. Submit your own contact form and confirm the message arrives where you would see it. Then open your site on your phone and time how long it takes to find and tap your phone number. Most problems surface in those three minutes.</p>

<h3>Do I need a new website or just changes to the one I have?</h3>
<p>Usually changes. A rebuild makes sense when the site is on a platform you cannot control, cannot be made fast, or has no page structure to build on. Otherwise, fixing speed, shortening the form, and adding real service pages gets you most of the result without the disruption of starting over.</p>

<h3>How many pages does a local service business site need?</h3>
<p>Enough to cover each service you actually want to sell and each town you actually want to work in, and no more. Ten strong pages outperform forty thin ones. Padding a site with content nobody searches for dilutes the pages that matter.</p>

<h3>Does any of this matter if I get most work from referrals?</h3>
<p>It matters more. Referred customers look you up before they call, and your site is where they decide whether the referral was right. A slow, dated site with no proof on it costs you work you had already earned.</p>
