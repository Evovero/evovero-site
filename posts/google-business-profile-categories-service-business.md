<p>If you run a local service business, you already know the map pack is where a lot of calls start. I have looked at plenty of profiles for trades like concrete, tree service and towing, and the category field is one of the most common places I see money left on the table. It takes ten minutes to fix and costs nothing.</p>

<h2>Why Your Category Matters More Than Your Description</h2>

<p>Google's own help page on <a href="https://support.google.com/business/answer/7249669?hl=en">managing your business category</a> says categories tell customers what your business does and help you show up in relevant searches. It also says plainly that the categories you select affect your local ranking.</p>

<p>That ties into how Google describes local results in general. According to Google's explanation of <a href="https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091">how to improve your local ranking</a>, results are mainly based on relevance, distance and prominence. Relevance is how well your profile matches what someone is searching for. Your category is one of the clearest signals you control for that first factor.</p>

<p>Think of it from Google's side. Someone searches for a tree service, and Google has to decide which profiles match that need. A profile with a category that names the exact service gives it a direct answer. A profile with a vague category makes it guess.</p>

<h2>How to Choose Your Primary Category</h2>

<p>You get one primary category, and it should be the one that best describes your business. Google's guidance is to choose a specific option over a general one. The example on its help page is picking "Nail salon" rather than "Salon," and the same logic applies to the trades.</p>

<h3>Start with the work that pays the bills</h3>

<p>Do not pick the category that describes your dream business or the one service you want to grow. Pick the one that describes what most of your customers call you for. If most of your revenue comes from driveways and patios, your primary category should reflect concrete work, not a broad label that also covers a dozen other trades.</p>

<h3>Be specific, but only as specific as the list allows</h3>

<p>You cannot invent your own category. If the specific one you want is not on the list, Google says to choose a general one that fits. So the process is simple: search the list for the most precise match to your core service, and if nothing is exact, take the closest one that still describes you honestly.</p>

<h3>Look at how the businesses above you are set up</h3>

<p>Search for your main service in your town and open the profiles that show up in the top results. The categories are visible on each profile, and they tell you how businesses that are already winning have described themselves. You are not copying blindly. You are checking that you have not missed an obvious option.</p>

<h2>How to Use Additional Categories Without Hurting Yourself</h2>

<p>Additional categories exist so you can describe secondary services or departments. Google's guidance is not to add a category for every product or service, and to use a few from the list instead. The example it gives is a grocery store that adds "Bakery" and "Deli" next to its primary category.</p>

<p>For a service business, the same idea works like this. Say your primary category is the main trade you do. If you also have a distinct second line of work that customers genuinely hire you for, that can be an additional category. A tree company that also does stump removal as part of the same crew is a reasonable example, as long as a matching category exists on the list.</p>

<p>Here is the rule of thumb I use:</p>

<ul>
<li>Add a category only if you would happily take a call for it tomorrow.</li>
<li>Skip any category that describes a service you subcontract and do not control.</li>
<li>Skip any category that is only loosely related to your main work, even if it sounds good for search.</li>
<li>If you are unsure, leave it off. You can add it later.</li>
</ul>

<p>The temptation is to stack every category you can think of, hoping to show up for more searches. That tends to blur what your profile is about, which works against the relevance signal you were trying to build.</p>

<h2>What Your Category Can Unlock</h2>

<p>Google notes that your category may also unlock features. Its examples include booking buttons for health and beauty businesses and menus for restaurants. The exact features vary by category, so it is worth looking at your own dashboard after you change a category to see whether anything new appears.</p>

<h2>Changing a Category Safely</h2>

<p>Adding or editing a category may require you to verify your business again, according to Google's help page. That is worth knowing before you make changes on a busy week. The steps on that page are to open your Business Profile, select Edit profile, select Edit next to Business category, make your changes and select Save. To remove an additional category, select Remove next to it.</p>

<p>Because a change can trigger re-verification, I decide first and change it once. Bouncing between categories to test what happens only creates a verification headache.</p>

Prominence also depends on things like reviews, and Google says more reviews and positive ratings can help your local ranking.</p>

<h2>Common Category Mistakes I See</h2>

<h3>Choosing a broad label because it feels safe</h3>

<p>A general category feels like it covers more ground, but it also describes you less clearly. If a more precise option fits, use it.</p>

<h3>Copying a competitor without checking fit</h3>

<p>A competitor may offer services you do not. Using their category set can attach your profile to work you will not actually take on, which wastes calls and frustrates customers.</p>

<h3>Never revisiting it</h3>

<p>Categories are easy to set once and forget. If your business has shifted toward a different mix of work, your profile should shift with it. Review it whenever your services change.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>

<h3>How many categories should a local service business use?</h3>

<p>Use one primary category and only a few additional ones. Google's guidance is not to add a category for every service. Each additional category should describe real work you want calls for. If a category is only loosely related, leave it off and keep the profile focused.</p>

<h3>Does my primary category affect where I show up?</h3>

<p>Yes. Google states that the categories you select affect your local ranking. Your primary category is where you state what you do, so choose the most specific accurate option. It works alongside distance, prominence and the rest of your profile information.</p>

<h3>Can I create my own category if mine is missing?</h3>

<p>No. Google says you cannot create your own category. If the exact one you want is not available, choose a general category that fits your business. Then use your services, description and photos to make the specifics clear to customers.</p>

<h3>Will changing my category require verification again?</h3>

<p>It might. Google notes that adding or editing a category may require you to verify your business again. Plan the change ahead of time, make it only once, and be ready to complete verification again if Google asks you to do so.</p>

<h3>Should I copy the categories of the top competitor?</h3>

<p>Use them as a reference, not a template. Checking what top profiles use can reveal an option you missed. But your categories should match the work you really do, so only keep the ones that honestly describe your business and the jobs you actually want.</p>
