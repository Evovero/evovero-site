<p>The marketing that works for a house cleaning business is different from the marketing that works for most trades, because the money is not in the job. It is in the client who books every two weeks for three years. So the goal of every ad, every page and every phone call is not a sale. It is a recurring client.</p>
<p>That one difference should change what you spend on, what you measure, and what you say when the phone rings. Here is how I would build it.</p>
<h2>Recurring clients are the whole business</h2>
<p>A one time deep clean and a biweekly recurring client can cost the same to acquire and are worth nothing like the same amount. If you treat them as equal leads, your numbers will tell you lies about which marketing is working.</p>
<p>Two practical consequences. First, track leads by what they turn into, not just by how many came in. A channel producing move out cleans only can look excellent on lead count and mediocre on revenue. Second, write your offers and your website copy for someone choosing a cleaner for the next few years, not someone solving a problem this weekend. Those are different readers with different questions, and the recurring one cares far more about trust, consistency and who is actually coming into the house.</p>
<h2>Your Google Business Profile is the highest leverage free thing you own</h2>
<p>Most cleaning businesses are service area businesses. You go to the client, the client does not come to you. Google has specific rules for that, and getting them wrong is a common cause of suspension.</p>
<p>If you do not have a public storefront that customers can walk into, your address has to be hidden on the profile. You still give Google a real address for verification, and Google uses it to work out proximity, but it does not display. PO boxes and virtual offices are against the guidelines and are a fast route to losing the profile. Google also expects your listed service area to be reasonable, generally not stretching beyond roughly two hours of driving from where you actually operate.</p>
<p>Once the profile is compliant, the ordinary work matters more than any trick. Complete every field. List your services individually rather than as one blob. Keep photos current and recognizably yours. Answer questions in the Q and A section yourself before someone else answers them for you. This is the same groundwork behind all <a href="/services/seo/">local SEO work</a>, and for a cleaning business it usually outperforms anything else per hour spent.</p>
<h2>Get found for the searches that actually convert</h2>
<p>People searching for a cleaner rarely type the phrase you would put on a business card. They search for a service plus a place, or a very specific situation.</p>
<p>That is an argument for having real pages rather than one page that lists everything. A page for recurring house cleaning, a page for move in and move out cleaning, a page for deep cleaning, and separate pages for the towns you serve. Each one can answer the questions that particular searcher has, which is what earns the click and the call.</p>
<p>Write them for a person rather than for a keyword. What is included and what is not. Whether you bring supplies. Whether it is the same person each visit. How access works when nobody is home. Those are the questions that decide whether someone books, and almost nobody answers them properly on their site.</p>
<h2>Paid traffic, and a change worth knowing about this year</h2>
<p>Search ads work for cleaning because the intent is strong. Someone typing "house cleaning" plus their town is not browsing. The mistakes I see most often are bidding on broad terms that pull in commercial and janitorial searches, sending every click to the homepage, and having no negative keyword list at all. Fixing those three things usually does more than raising the budget.</p>
<p>Local Services Ads are the other option worth understanding, and house cleaning is one of the covered categories. They sit above the regular search results, carry a Google verification badge, and bill per lead rather than per click.</p>
<p>The thing to know right now is that Google is moving Local Services Ads into the main Google Ads platform, using a Performance Max campaign type built for pay per lead goals. The first phase began in August 2026 for selected United States home and storefront service advertisers, house cleaning among them. Service area businesses without a storefront and accounts with custom bidding or booking setups are slated for later in 2026, with remaining categories and non United States accounts following in 2027. Google says it will email a business roughly two weeks before its account is migrated. If you run Local Services Ads, watch for that email and export your historical lead data before the interface changes. Practical help with <a href="/services/google-ads/">Google Ads management</a> matters more than usual during a transition like this one.</p>
<h2>The lead you already have is cheaper than the next one</h2>
<p>Cleaning is an emotional purchase as much as a practical one. Someone is deciding whether to let a stranger into their home. They tend to call two or three companies, and they very often go with whoever picks up and sounds trustworthy.</p>
<p>So the cheapest improvement available to most cleaning businesses is not more leads. It is answering the ones they already get. If you are on a job, you are not answering the phone, and a voicemail box is where a booking goes to die. An <a href="/services/ai-automation/">AI receptionist</a> that answers every call, captures the details and books or hands off is not a luxury for a business where the owner is physically working most of the week.</p>
<p>Whatever you use, respond fast. The gap between a five minute callback and a next day callback is enormous, and it costs nothing to close.</p>
<h2>Reviews are your entire trust argument</h2>
<p>You are asking for a key to someone's house. Reviews are how a stranger decides that is reasonable.</p>
<p>Ask for them in person at the end of a visit and follow up with a link, ideally the same day. Ask every client, not just the delighted ones, because a steady flow of ordinary reviews reads as more credible than a burst of glowing ones. Never offer anything in exchange for a review. Reply to all of them, especially the critical ones, because the reply is aimed at the next reader rather than at the reviewer. Ongoing <a href="/services/reputation/">reputation management</a> for a cleaning business is mostly just doing this consistently instead of in bursts.</p>
<h2>Own what you build</h2>
<p>Lead marketplaces will sell you cleaning leads, and the same lead is usually sold to several companies at once. You compete on speed and price, you never own the relationship, and the day you stop paying, the work stops.</p>
<p>A website, a Google Business Profile, a review base and a list of past clients are assets that keep producing. They take longer to build. They are also still yours in three years. For a business model built on recurring revenue, that alignment is the point.</p>
<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<h3>Should a cleaning business use Google Ads or Local Services Ads?</h3>
<p>Both can work and they do different jobs. Local Services Ads bill per lead and sit at the very top of the page with a verification badge. Regular search ads give you more control over keywords and landing pages. Many businesses run both, then keep whichever produces more recurring clients rather than more raw leads.</p>
<h3>Do I have to show my home address on Google?</h3>
<p>No, and you should not. If customers do not come to you, Google requires the address to be hidden and the profile to be set up as a service area business. You still provide a verifiable address privately. Using a PO box or a virtual office instead breaks the guidelines and risks suspension.</p>
<h3>How many reviews do I need?</h3>
<p>There is no threshold that flips a switch. What matters is that reviews are recent, steady and specific, because a profile whose newest review is two years old reads as a business that may not still be operating. Build the request into the end of every job and the number takes care of itself.</p>
<h3>Is social media worth it for a cleaning business?</h3>
<p>It is a supporting channel, not a primary one. People looking for a cleaner search for one, they do not scroll for one. Social is useful for staying visible to past clients, showing your team and collecting referrals, which suits a recurring model. I would get search and the phone answered right before putting real effort there.</p>
<h3>What is the single highest return thing to fix first?</h3>
<p>Answering the phone, then the Google Business Profile. Neither costs much. Both directly affect whether the leads you already generate turn into recurring clients, and there is no point increasing the top of the funnel while calls are going to voicemail.</p>
