<p>The way to get more Google reviews is simple to describe and rare to execute: ask every customer at the moment the job wraps up, make leaving a review a one-tap action, and do it consistently every week. Most service businesses lose reviews not because customers refuse, but because nobody asks, or the ask arrives three weeks late with no link attached.</p>
<p>I work with local service businesses every day, and reviews come up in almost every conversation. The owners with 15 reviews and the owners with 150 are usually doing the same quality of work. The difference is a system. Here is how to build one, including the Google policy changes from this year that you need to know before you copy an older playbook.</p>
<h2>Why Reviews Decide Who Gets the Call</h2>
<p>When a homeowner searches for a concrete contractor or a tree service, Google shows a map with three businesses on it. Review signals are among the strongest factors deciding who lands in that map pack: how many reviews you have, your average rating, how recently reviews have come in, and what the reviews actually say. Recency matters more than most owners realize. A steady trickle of new reviews every month signals an active business, while a profile that went quiet a year ago slowly loses ground even if its lifetime count is high.</p>
<p>Reviews also do the selling before you ever pick up the phone. A prospect comparing two contractors with similar work will call the one whose reviews mention showing up on time, clean job sites, and fair dealing. That text inside the reviews feeds both Google's ranking and the customer's decision. It is the cheapest marketing asset you can build, and it compounds. <h2>The System: Ask, Link, Follow Up Once</h2>
<h3>Ask at the moment of finished work</h3>
<p>The best time to ask is right when the job is done and the customer is standing in front of the result. Satisfaction peaks at completion. A simple verbal line works: "If you're happy with how this turned out, a Google review really helps a small business like ours." That sentence, said in person, roughly doubles the odds that the follow-up text gets acted on.</p>
<h3>Send the link the same day</h3>
<p>The verbal ask plants the seed; the link makes it happen. Google gives every Business Profile a short review link, and in 2026 it added a built-in QR code generator right inside the profile dashboard, so you can create both without any third-party tool. Text the link the same day: "Thanks again for choosing us. Here's the direct link if you'd like to leave that review." One tap opens the review box. Every step you remove raises the completion rate.</p>
<h3>Put the link everywhere passive</h3>
<p>Beyond the direct ask, the review link belongs in your email signature, on your invoices, and on the back of your business card as a QR code. These passive placements catch customers weeks later, when they are paying the bill or passing your card to a neighbor. None of it requires you to remember anything, which is exactly why it works.</p>
<h3>Follow up exactly once</h3>
<p>People forget. One reminder, three to five days later, is polite and effective. Two reminders is nagging. Build the single follow-up into your process and then let it go. If you use any kind of job management software or automated texting, this is a perfect thing to automate; it is the same kind of follow-through <a href="https://www.evovero.com/services/ai-automation/">automation handles well</a> for missed calls and estimate follow-ups.</p>
<h2>What Changed in Google's Review Rules This Year</h2>
<p>Google tightened its review policies in early 2026, and some tactics that agencies recommended for years are now explicitly against the rules. If you copy an older guide, you can get reviews removed or your profile penalized. The current lines to respect:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>No incentives.</strong> Discounts, gift cards, or freebies in exchange for reviews have long been banned, and enforcement is real. The review has to be freely given.</li>
<li><strong>No review gating.</strong> You cannot survey customers first and only send happy ones to Google. Every customer gets the same pathway. Penalties for gating now hit the whole profile, not just individual reviews.</li>
<li><strong>No staff quotas or scripted content.</strong> The 2026 update explicitly prohibits requiring employees to collect a set number of reviews and prohibits asking customers to include specific content, including a staff member's name.</li>
<li><strong>No on-site kiosks.</strong> Collecting reviews from a tablet at your counter is out; reviews left in bulk from one location look fake to Google because they often are.</li>
</ul>
<p>None of this hurts an honest operation. Ask everyone, make it easy, let customers say what they want. The businesses these rules punish are the ones manufacturing their reputation instead of earning it.</p>
<h2>Respond to Every Review, Especially the Bad Ones</h2>
<p>Responding to reviews is the half of reputation work most owners skip. A short, personal reply to a five-star review thanks the customer and shows prospects that a real person runs the business. The negative ones matter even more. Respond calmly, acknowledge the concern, and offer to make it right offline. Never argue in public. A prospect reading a measured, professional response to an unfair review learns more about how you handle problems than fifty five-star ratings could tell them.</p>
<p>One caution: do not fire back the same hour you read a bad review. Write the response, sit on it, and post it once the frustration has passed. The response is for the next thousand readers, not for the one reviewer.</p>
<h2>Make It a Weekly Habit, Not a Campaign</h2>
<p>The most common failure mode is the review blitz: an owner reads an article like this one, sends twenty requests in a weekend, gets six reviews, and never asks again. Google's algorithm favors consistency over bursts, and a sudden spike followed by silence looks worse than a steady flow. A realistic target for most service businesses is a handful of new reviews each month, sourced from that week's completed jobs.</p>
<p>Put it on the calendar: every Friday, text the review link to that week's finished customers. Fifteen minutes a week builds a review profile that competitors doing occasional blitzes can never catch. This rhythm works in every trade I serve, from <a href="https://www.evovero.com/industries/">concrete and tree service to home cleaning</a>, because the mechanics of a happy customer are the same everywhere.</p>
<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<h3>How do I find my Google review link?</h3>
<p>Open your Google Business Profile dashboard, look for the "Ask for reviews" option, and copy the short link Google generates. In 2026 Google also added a built-in QR code you can download from the same place, sized for print on invoices and cards. No third-party tool is needed.</p>
<h3>Can I ask every customer for a review?</h3>
<p>Yes, and you should. Asking is fully allowed; what is banned is paying for reviews, gating out unhappy customers, setting staff quotas, or telling customers what to write. Ask everyone the same way, make the link easy, and let the words be theirs.</p>
<h3>What should I do about a fake or unfair review?</h3>
<p>Flag it through your Business Profile for removal if it violates Google's policies, then post a calm public response either way, since removal can take time and is not guaranteed. State the facts briefly, avoid a back-and-forth, and let your other reviews provide the context.</p>
<h3>Do Google reviews actually help my ranking?</h3>
<p>Yes. Review volume, average rating, recency, and the text inside reviews all feed Google's local ranking systems, and industry analysis consistently places review signals among the strongest map pack factors. Reviews work alongside your website and profile optimization rather than replacing either one.</p>
<h3>How many reviews do I need?</h3>
<p>There is no magic number; what matters is beating the businesses you compete against locally and keeping fresh reviews arriving. Check the top three profiles in your market for your main search, note their counts and ratings, and build a steady weekly asking habit until you clear them.</p>
