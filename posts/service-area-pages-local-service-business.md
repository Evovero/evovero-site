<p>A service area page works when it gives someone in that town a real reason to call you: proof you work there, details that apply to their area, and a clear next step. It fails when it is the same page copied thirty times with the town name swapped. Google treats that second version as a doorway page, and it can drag down the rest of your site. The fix is fewer, better pages built from real information.</p>

<p>I build websites for local service businesses, and town pages come up in almost every project. Owners want to show up in the towns they serve, which is the right goal. The question is how to get there without building pages that search engines discount and customers ignore.</p>

<h2>Why Service Area Pages Exist at All</h2>
<p>If you pour concrete, haul cars or clean houses across a region, people search with their own town in mind. A homeowner types the service plus the town name, and Google tries to show businesses that clearly serve that place. Your Google Business Profile carries a lot of that weight for map results. Your website carries it for the regular results below the map, and a page focused on a town gives Google something concrete to match.</p>
<p>A good town page also helps the person reading it. Someone who lands on a page about their town and sees work, details and answers that fit their situation is far more likely to pick up the phone than someone reading a generic services page.</p>

<h2>What Google Says About Doorway Pages</h2>
<p>This is where many sites go wrong. Google's <a href="https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies">spam policies for web search</a> describe doorway abuse as sites or pages created to rank for specific, similar search queries. One of the examples Google lists is having multiple domain names or pages targeted at specific regions or cities that funnel users to one page. Another is creating substantially similar pages that are closer to search results than a clearly defined, browseable hierarchy.</p>
<p>The same policy page also covers scaled content abuse, which it describes as many pages generated for the primary purpose of manipulating search rankings and not helping users. That applies whether the pages were written by hand, by a template or by an AI tool.</p>
<p>Put plainly: a town page is fine. Thirty near-identical town pages that exist only to catch searches are the problem.</p>

<h2>The Copy and Paste Trap</h2>
<p>Here is the version I see most often when I audit a site. The business has a page for every town within an hour's drive. Each page has the same three paragraphs, the same photos and the same list of services. The only difference is the town name, dropped into the heading and a couple of sentences.</p>
<p>That approach has three costs:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>It reads as thin to search engines.</strong> Pages that are nearly identical give Google no reason to rank one over another, and they fit the doorway description above.</li>
<li><strong>It reads as thin to people.</strong> A customer who clicks two of your town pages and sees the same text knows the page was not written for them.</li>
<li><strong>It spreads your effort.</strong> Time spent publishing forty weak pages is time not spent on the ten towns where most of your jobs actually come from.</li>
</ul>

<h2>What Makes a Town Page Worth Having</h2>
<p>Every strong service area page I build answers one question: what would a customer in this town want to know before calling? The answers come from real information, not filler.</p>
<h3>Proof you work there</h3>
<p>Photos of jobs you have done in or near that town, with honest captions, are the strongest signal you can offer. If you do not know where a photo was taken, do not label it with a town. A general description is better than a claim you cannot back up.</p>
<h3>Local details that change the job</h3>
<p>For a contractor, this might be the city's permit process, soil or drainage conditions, or the kinds of homes common in that area. For a towing company, it might be the main roads and highways you cover. Pull these from official sources like the city's own website, and link to them when you can. Never make them up.</p>
<h3>Answers to the questions people actually ask</h3>
<p>A short FAQ written for that town, with plain answers, helps the reader and gives the page substance. Keep the answers specific and honest.</p>
<h3>A clear next step</h3>
<p>Your phone number, a short form and a sentence about how to reach you belong on every town page. The goal is a call or an estimate request, not a long read.</p>

<h2>How Many Town Pages Should You Build?</h2>
<p>Start with the towns where you already do the most work and want more of it. For most local service businesses that is a short list, not every town on the map. Build those pages well, watch which ones bring calls, and add more only when you have something real to say about the next town.</p>
<p>If a town is small or you rarely work there, it can be covered on a broader service areas page that lists the places you serve. That page helps customers check coverage without creating a pile of thin pages.</p>

<h2>Connect Town Pages to the Rest of Your Site</h2>
<p>A town page should not sit on its own. Link it to the matching service pages, and link to it from them, so Google can see how your site fits together and visitors can move from "do they work here" to "do they do what I need." Blog posts that cover a local topic can link to the town page as well. That kind of clear, browseable structure is the opposite of the doorway pattern Google describes.</p>
<p>Your website and your Google Business Profile should also tell the same story. If your profile lists the areas you serve, your town pages should match. I covered that side in my post on <a href="/blog/google-business-profile-service-area/">setting up your Google Business Profile service area</a>.</p>

<h2>A Quick Checklist Before You Publish</h2>
<ol>
<li>Could this page only have been written for this town?</li>
<li>Does it include real photos or proof of work, honestly labeled?</li>
<li>Are any local facts pulled from an official source?</li>
<li>Does it answer questions a customer in this town would ask?</li>
<li>Is there a phone number and a clear way to request an estimate?</li>
<li>Does it link to and from the matching service pages?</li>
</ol>
<p>If a page fails the first question, it probably should not exist yet.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>

<h3>Are service area pages bad for SEO?</h3>
<p>No. Service area pages are useful when each one offers something specific to that town, such as real work, local details and answers to local questions. They become a problem when they are near copies with only the town name changed, because Google's spam policies describe that pattern as doorway abuse.</p>

<h3>How long should a service area page be?</h3>
<p>Long enough to be useful and no longer. A strong town page covers proof of work, local details, a few common questions and a clear way to get in touch. Length matters far less than whether the content could only have been written for that town.</p>

<h3>Can I use AI to write my town pages?</h3>
<p>You can use tools to help draft, but the facts and proof have to be real and specific to each town. Google's spam policies describe scaled content abuse as many pages generated to manipulate rankings rather than help users, however they are produced. Edit every page and check every local claim.</p>

<h3>Should I make a page for every town I could serve?</h3>
<p>Start with the towns where you do the most work and want more. Cover smaller or occasional areas on one service areas page instead. Add individual town pages over time, when you have real work and useful local information to put on them.</p>
